LLM-as-a-judge · measurement study

Does packing criteria
into one judge call
quietly degrade them?

You pay for your judge to read the full input once. Stack judges for multiple quality dimensions and you re-pay that read every time. The obvious fix is to score everything in one call — and the obvious worry is that something breaks when you do.

01 · the cost structure

You pay to read the input every time

A judge cannot score anything until it has read the prompt and the response. Score five dimensions in five calls and you buy that read five times. The content is identical each time. Only the question changes.

02 · why this is not obvious

Two hypotheses, both plausible

Packing hurts — divided attention

Attention is split across criteria. One shared reasoning budget now covers five judgements instead of one. Each score gets less of the model than it used to. Per-criterion accuracy declines as group size rises.

Packing helps — criteria in context

The judge sees the whole rubric at once and can trade dimensions off coherently rather than scoring each in isolation. A response is verbose relative to how helpful it is. Splitting the call hides that.

Pre-registered rival: any apparent effect is really about where a criterion sits in the prompt, not how many share the call. That implies a different fix — order your criteria — so it is modelled alongside, not treated as a nuisance.

03 · the design decision that shapes everything

Why not judge versus judge

The cheap way to answer this is to run a packed judge against an unpacked one and measure disagreement. That measures agreement between two fallible instruments, and it cannot tell you which one moved. If both degrade together it reports a clean null.

  • So: ground truth on both sides. Human labels where they exist, corruption applied by construction where they do not.
  • HelpSteer2 gives the human side: 10,681 prompts × 2 responses, five criteria, Likert 0–4, at least three annotators each. Two responses per prompt gives pairwise ground truth with a clean 0.5 baseline.
  • The closest published result leans pessimistic — LongJudgeBench found a rubric added on top of a reference gave no improvement (0.578 vs 0.584). But that varied whether a rubric was present, not how many criteria shared a call.

05 · why you should believe it

What is held constant

  • The criteria themselves. Every condition scores all of them. Only the packing changes.
  • Three randomisations, all required. Partition membership, criterion order within the call, and response order — each seeded from SHA-256(base_seed, item_id, nominal_n, replicate). Drop any one and a group-size effect is confounded with criterion pairing or with position.
  • Per-criterion output budget. Content budget is 40 + 220 × group_size. If a five-criterion call had to emit five scores and five rationales under a one-criterion ceiling, accuracy would drop for a completely uninteresting reason and I would have measured my own token limit.

Had the effect vanished when that control went in, that would have been the finding. Report it and stop. Acknowledged and uncontrolled: a five-criterion prompt is genuinely longer, so this is not a pure test of divided attention and is not described as one.

06 · the outcome nobody reports

The tie rate is co-primary, and it is flat

When the judge scores both responses equally it has expressed no preference. Those observations have no correct answer, so they leave the accuracy outcome. Excluding them is only defensible if you report them.

The trap

If packing makes a judge flatter and more indecisive, that is degradation — and dropping ties silently hides it, biasing the contrast in exactly the direction you were hoping to find.

What happened

0.4800 → 0.4796. Resolution does not vary with treatment, so conditioning on resolved observations is not a collider here. The selection concern is discharged empirically rather than argued away.

Report it next to accuracy, always. A judge that stops committing looks identical to a judge that got better, if you only ever look at the rows where it committed.

07 · why you should believe it

Estimators tested against a known answer

  • Clustered standard errors, always. Observations cluster by source prompt. Clustered SEs have been measured at over 3× naive ones on comparable eval data. Naive SEs appear nowhere in this work.
  • Within-item paired contrasts as primary. Every item is judged at every group size, so item difficulty differences out exactly rather than being modelled — roughly a third of the variance at ρ = 0.5.
  • Three estimators reported together, because they fail differently: paired contrast, cluster-robust logistic regression, and a crossed mixed model with random intercepts for item and criterion. All three agree here.
  • And the suite is validated against planted effects. It recovers a known degradation with the correct sign and significance, and produces no significant curve on a judge with nothing planted.

An estimator suite that has never been tested against a known answer is not evidence of anything. Plant an effect. Check you find it. Plant nothing. Check you do not.

09 · what it took to measure this

Your difficulty classification will be wrong

I sorted twenty corruption operations into surface (detectable without comprehension) and semantic (requires reading and reasoning), then measured them. Eight of twenty contradicted the class I had assigned.

  • Only 5 of 12 operations predicted to sit at ceiling actually reached it.
  • contradict — hypothesised semantic and difficult — reached 0.93.
  • jargon is mute: lexical jargon substitution does not move this judge's complexity score at any severity tested. That is a finding about the judge — its notion of complexity tracks conceptual depth, not vocabulary.

The classification was pre-committed as a hypothesis, and measurement governs. This is why the calibration gate exists at all: it runs every operation at group size 1 before a penny is spent on the sweep, and it reassigns them by what they actually do.

10 · what it took to measure this

Detection rate hides two different failures

A low detection rate can mean the judge judged backwards, or that it saw no difference at all. Those need opposite remedies, and one number cannot tell them apart.

detection

hits / n

tie rate

ties / n

discrimination

hits / (hits + wrong) — accuracy given the judge committed

  • Wrong-direction rates were near zero across all twenty operations while tie rates reached 0.95. Every low-detection operation was insensitive, not inaccurate.
  • A rule treating detection below 0.60 as near-chance would have thrown out five operations that were directionally perfect and simply needed more severity. Chance looks like 50% wrong; these were 0–10%.

And severity is not monotone in validity. Raising it on the five insensitive operations improved one and damaged three — so it needs a ladder, not a guess. vague reversed direction outright and was removed.

11 · how far it pushes

Past the labelled ceiling

Five is where the labels stop. No public corpus offers more than about five fixed, fully-labelled criteria per item — FLASK annotates three of twelve skills; BiGGen uses per-instance rubrics, so criteria differ across items and cannot be packed at all. A null at five with no located breaking point is weak evidence.

  • Corruption ground truth is not bound by that ceiling. Seed a known edit with a known direction and you have a label for free. Fifteen synthetic-only criteria join the five anchors — K = 20, 17 operations measured, three load-only.
  • The estimand narrows to match. Did the targeted score move in the seeded direction? Ties and wrong directions are both misses.
  • Conclusions attach to operations, not criteria. I can speak about detecting injected typos and recovering a deleted answer. Not about judging verbosity the way a human does.

Leakage is reported, not used as a gate. Deleting an answer sentence necessarily hurts coherence too. That is a fact about text, and excluding operations because their observed leakage looked bad would be selecting on the outcome.

12 · how far it pushes

The K = 20 sweep

10,880 calls · endpoints of the swept ladder

  • Semantic operations did not decline (70.5 → 72.0). The class that was supposed to break under load is the one that held.
  • Surface operations fell (78.8 → 68.3) — the class predicted to sit at ceiling and stay there.
  • Neither contrast survives Holm correction, and both movements sit inside the shaded band. Which is the honest headline of this slide.

13 · the limit

What this study could not have seen

~4 pts

MDE at K = 5

2.87×

observation imbalance in the sweep

The two halves of this talk are not equally powered, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. The K = 5 result has an interval you can act on. The K = 20 sweep does not.

  • Every movement in the sweep sits inside the band it could resolve. Absence of a significant effect there is not evidence of absence.
  • The surface decline has two candidate explanations — load, or position in the prompt — and this design cannot separate them. Position was pre-registered as the rival so the reading could not be built after seeing the curve.
  • Detection class is a property of the operation, not the criterion. Separating the two needs a second operation of the opposite class on the same criterion. Not implemented, so not claimed.

14 · the defensible claim

What I will actually claim

On human-labelled data, packing five criteria into one judge call changed accuracy by +0.005 (95% CI −0.034 to +0.043) while cutting cost 46%. Extending the load to twenty criteria with synthetic corruption showed no degradation that survives correction — but was not powered to find one smaller than 13 points.

And what I am not claiming

  • Not that packing is safe at twenty. That it was not shown to be unsafe is a weaker statement, and it is the one the data supports.
  • Not that each corruption isolates a human quality dimension.
  • Not generalisation past this judge and this corpus. Per-dataset variance across published benchmark suites is large enough that judges need validating per use case.

15 · takeaway

If you run judges in production

  • Pack them, up to about five. That range is measured, the saving is metered, and the interval is tight. Past that you are extrapolating — as am I.
  • Anchor every scale point. Skip one and the judge quietly adopts a shorter scale and ties on two thirds of your pairs.
  • Report the tie rate next to accuracy. A judge that stops committing is degrading, and dropping ties hides it in the direction you want to find.
  • Hold the per-criterion output budget constant, or you will measure your own token ceiling and call it divided attention.
  • Randomise criterion order, and plant an effect in your estimators before you trust them on real data.
